## 🧭 About Me

I build software that makes difficult ideas **inspectable rather than magical**.

Most of my work follows the same experiment, run twice: take a body of theory — calculus,
probability, financial mathematics — implement it **from first principles instead of importing a
library**, then build an interface that makes the theory legible to someone who has never read the
textbook.

That constraint is deliberate. Calling `scipy.stats.norm.ppf` teaches you nothing. Writing the
probit function yourself as a rational approximation refined by a Halley step — then proving
`Φ(Φ⁻¹(p)) = p` to ten decimal places in a unit test — teaches you what the function actually *is*.

**How I think about problems**

- **Correctness must be visible, not assumed.** Both of my platforms display a mathematical identity
  live on screen that must hold exactly. If the math breaks, the page says so instead of quietly
  showing a plausible-looking number.
- **Test against something external.** A test that compares a function to itself proves nothing.
  Mine compare symbolic derivatives against finite differences, and Monte Carlo estimates against
  their closed-form answers.
- **Never block the user.** Half a million simulated paths run in a Web Worker; where a worker is
  unavailable, the same batched runner yields one batch per animation frame. The page never freezes.

**Why this direction**

I want to study Computer Science because the most interesting problems I have met are the ones where
software and mathematics stop being separate subjects. A savings plan is a pension valuation. An AP
Calculus chain rule is an option Greek. I build tools that make those connections obvious to the
person learning them — because that connection is the thing nobody taught me, and I had to build it
myself to see it.

## 🚀 Project Showcase

### 🧮 MathForge — SAT/AP Calculus Learning Platform

[![Live](https://img.shields.io/badge/Live_Demo-8957e5?style=flat-square&logo=vercel&logoColor=white)](https://mathforge-blush.vercel.app/)
[![Source](https://img.shields.io/badge/Source_Code-181717?style=flat-square&logo=github&logoColor=white)](https://github.com/dung037517-netizen/mathforge)
![Tests](https://img.shields.io/badge/tests-48_passing-3fb950?style=flat-square&logo=vitest&logoColor=white)
![Deps](https://img.shields.io/badge/runtime_math_dependencies-0-f78166?style=flat-square)

> **Situation** — Every online calculus solver I used as a student gave me the answer and hid the
> reasoning. That is precisely backwards for exam preparation: on the AP exam, the method carries
> the marks. Students end up able to produce answers they cannot reproduce under test conditions.
>
> **Task** — Build a platform that teaches the *derivation*, and do it without a computer-algebra
> library, so that every step is something I understood well enough to implement myself.
>
> **Action** — I hand-wrote the entire computation engine in strict TypeScript with zero uses of
> `any`:
> - A **recursive-descent parser** with implicit multiplication and character-level error positions,
>   so a malformed input points a caret at the exact offending character.
> - A **symbolic differentiator** that names every rule it applies — product, quotient, chain,
>   generalised power, and logarithmic differentiation for cases like `xˣ`.
> - **Adaptive Simpson quadrature** with Richardson extrapolation for definite integrals.
> - Dense linear algebra: **LU with partial pivoting**, Gauss-Jordan inversion, cyclic **Jacobi
>   eigenvalues**.
> - A **Central Limit Theorem simulator** running in a dedicated **Web Worker**, with a cooperative
>   main-thread scheduler as fallback so the UI never freezes at any path count.
>
> **Result** — 12 exam-style AP Calculus AB/BC and AP Statistics problems, each solved with a
> complete derivation. **48 unit tests** pin the engine against closed-form results rather than
> against itself. The CLT panel reproduces the predicted `σ/√n` to within **0.70%** live on screen,
> and the page reaches interactive in **~0.95 s** with 25 formulas already typeset. Every AP topic
> is annotated with the professional technique it becomes.

### 📈 FinHub Tracker — Student Finance Planning Web App

[![Live](https://img.shields.io/badge/Live_Demo-1f6feb?style=flat-square&logo=vercel&logoColor=white)](https://finhubtracker-maudung.vercel.app/)
[![Source](https://img.shields.io/badge/Source_Code-181717?style=flat-square&logo=github&logoColor=white)](https://github.com/dung037517-netizen/financeflow)
![Tests](https://img.shields.io/badge/tests-63_passing-3fb950?style=flat-square&logo=vitest&logoColor=white)
![Monte Carlo](https://img.shields.io/badge/Monte_Carlo-1200_futures-8957e5?style=flat-square)

> **Situation** — Planning to study abroad forced me to confront a genuinely hard question my family
> could not answer with a spreadsheet: given tuition inflation, market volatility, and four years of
> costs, *what is the actual probability we run out of money?*
>
> **Task** — Model a college savings plan the way an actuary models a pension — because structurally
> it **is** a pension in miniature: an accumulation phase, a decumulation phase, an inflation
> assumption, and a solvency question at the end.
>
> **Action** — I built the planning engine from scratch, with no financial libraries:
> - Costs inflated forward geometrically; savings accumulated as an **annuity-due** with monthly
>   compounding, then drawn down semester by semester as bills land.
> - Because contributions and withdrawals overlap, **no closed form exists** for the required
>   payment — so I solved it by **bisection on the minimum balance**, a quantity provably monotone
>   in the contribution, meaning the search cannot diverge.
> - **1,200-path Monte Carlo** over lognormal returns produces percentile fan bands, shortfall
>   probability, expected shortfall, and VaR/TVaR.
> - Underneath sits a full risk desk: Value at Risk by four methods (historical, parametric,
>   Cornish-Fisher, Monte Carlo) and **Black-Scholes-Merton** with an eight-Greek surface.
>
> **Result** — **63 unit tests**, including one that pins the savings ledger against the annuity-due
> closed form and one that verifies money conservation. The model surfaced a genuinely
> counter-intuitive finding: **funding a plan to its *expected* return still leaves roughly a 50%
> chance of falling short**, because the median lognormal path sits below the mean. Reaching 90%
> confidence costs **36–44% more per month** — which is exactly what an actuarial risk margin is.
> The option pricer displays its put-call parity residual live; it holds to **3.6 × 10⁻¹¹**.

## 🌱 Initiative & Leadership

### Open-Sourcing Both Platforms for Other Students

> **Situation** — The tools I built solved my own problems, but the underlying problems are not
> unique to me: classmates preparing for AP exams had the same complaint about answer-only solvers,
> and families applying abroad had the same uncertainty about cost planning.
>
> **Task** — Turn two personal tools into something another student could actually pick up, trust,
> and learn from — including the parts most student projects skip.
>
> **Action** — I treated documentation and verifiability as first-class deliverables, not
> afterthoughts: architecture diagrams, an explicit written mapping from each AP topic to its
> professional counterpart, and a stated policy that every numerical claim in the READMEs is
> reproducible from a fixed random seed. I deliberately used **synthetic market data with fictitious
> tickers** rather than scraped real prices, and said so plainly — because implying a market-data
> licence I do not hold would be dishonest, and because seeded data makes every figure I publish
> independently checkable.
>
> **Result** — Both repositories ship with zero build errors, zero lint warnings, zero uses of
> `any`, and **111 passing tests** between them. Both are deployed publicly and load in under a
> second. Anyone can clone either repo, run `npm test`, and confirm that the mathematics I claim is
> the mathematics that runs.

### Self-Directed Study in Applied Mathematics

> **Situation** — The mathematics I wanted to implement — survival models, option pricing, coherent
> risk measures — sits well beyond a standard high school syllabus, and no course was going to cover
> it for me.
>
> **Task** — Learn enough numerical analysis and probability theory to implement these correctly,
> and prove to myself that I had, rather than assuming it.
>
> **Action** — I worked through the theory and implemented each piece from its definition: Lanczos
> log-gamma, the regularised incomplete gamma function by series and continued fraction, `erf`/`erfc`
> derived from it, and the probit function via rational approximation with a Halley refinement. For
> each one I wrote tests comparing against independently known values — not against my own output.
>
> **Result** — A verified special-function library accurate to twelve decimal places, built without
> reference to an existing implementation. More usefully, I now understand *why* the complementary
> error function must be evaluated directly rather than as `1 − erf(x)`: the subtraction destroys
> precision exactly in the tail, which is the only region a risk calculation actually cares about.

## 🎯 What I Am Working Toward

I am applying to study **Computer Science** in the United States. The direction I care about is the
overlap between software engineering and applied mathematics — systems where correctness is
provable, performance is measurable, and the output has to be trustworthy because someone is going
to make a decision with it.

Concretely, I want to keep building things that:

- **Make expertise transferable.** A tool that shows its reasoning teaches; a tool that hides its
  reasoning creates dependence.
- **Are honest about uncertainty.** Reporting a single number when the honest answer is a
  distribution is a design failure, not a simplification.
- **Are verifiable by strangers.** Seeded randomness, closed-form test oracles, and on-screen
  identity checks, so that trust does not require taking my word for anything.
